# YouTube playbook (solo creator edition)

Distilled from Jake Trinder's "Give Me 15 Minutes and I'll Make You Disgustingly Good at YouTube", with the agency/sales parts translated for a person building a channel, and caveats where his advice doesn't transfer.

## The core model: a video is a series system

Topic → packaging → hook → body → edit. **Every stage must work, or the video fails.** Five decisions that are each 90% right give roughly a 59% chance the whole video works (0.9⁵). So the goal is not one brilliant step. It is **no weak link**. Each command below removes one single point of failure.

| Stage | Question it must answer | Command |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Idea | Will people *choose* to watch this? | `/idea-check` |
| 2. Packaging | Will they pick mine over 20 others in the feed? | `/package` |
| 3. Hook + outline | Does the video kill their doubt, then keep the promise? | `/outline` |
| 4. Edit | Does the edit match what the thumbnail promised? | `/edit-video` |
| 5. Results | What does the data tell me to look at next time? | `/post-mortem` |

All five read and write `projects/<name>/brief.md`, so every decision for a video lives in one place.

## 1. Idea: revealed preference

- People's choices beat what they say they want. Look at what your audience **already watches**, not what you think they need.
- For a topic, find the **floor** (median views of decent videos on it) and the **ceiling** (the best ones). Is the floor worth a week of your build time?
- Check that the demand is **current** (recent videos still getting views) and **real** (no obviously botted outliers).
- **Raise the ceiling by stacking demand:** attach the topic to something bigger that people already choose. His example was "Alex Hormozi's lead generation strategy". For a maker, that might be a famous character, a film moment or a well-known creator's build.

## 2. Packaging: copy patterns, not outliers

- One viral video is not a pattern. A pattern is a **framing that wins across several niches** (e.g. "The new rules of…").
- Always ask **why** a format works and whether that reason holds for *your* audience. Copying without the reason is overfitting.
- Thumbnail job 1: get picked out of the feed. Job 2: be understood in one glance. The title then closes part of the curiosity gap, not all of it.
- Gut check: **you know the idea is good when you feel nervous about messing it up.**

## 3. Hook and body

The hook answers four doubts fast (the four Ps):
1. **Promise:** is this what I clicked for?
2. **Proof:** why should I listen to you? For a solo maker, proof is the result on screen: the finished suit, the failure count, the hours.
3. **Plan:** how are you getting me there?
4. **Persona:** is this for me?

Body: write **acceptance criteria** from the title. Ask "what must be true by the end for this title to be honest?", then break each answer down until it's concrete. That list *is* the outline, and the video is done when every item passes.

Satisfaction beats retention. Viewers judge against **expectation**, so beat the expectation the packaging set.

## 4. Edit: match the promise

The most useful point for this tool: **a polished edit is not automatically better.** If the title and thumbnail promise raw and honest, heavy effects break the promise and can sink a good video. So `/edit-video` picks an edit intensity from the brief:
- **raw:** cut dead air and retakes only. Light captions, no b-roll spam.
- **standard:** add a few graphics, b-roll where something is described, music under the speech.
- **high-energy:** tight cuts, punch-in zooms, frequent b-roll, list and callout graphics.

## 5. Results: signals tell you where to look

- Best early signal of a breakout: **positive comment-to-view ratio**, plus what the comments actually say. Like-to-view and subs-per-view back it up.
- A low click-through rate means *look at* the thumbnail, not automatically change it. A 2–3% CTR can still work.
- A sharp retention drop means *look at that moment*. If it's a call to action, ramble or tangent, cut it from the next video (or trim the live one in YouTube Studio's editor).

## Where his advice doesn't transfer (sparring notes)

- **"12% retention was fine."** That was an 800k-view video selling a high-ticket service to buyers. A small channel needs the algorithm to test it widely first, and weak retention usually stops that. Treat it as survivorship, not a rule.
- **"Research needs an agency."** That's his sales pitch (bounded rationality, so hire the people who see the most data). A solo version is vidIQ, YouTube search and Studio's Research tab, which gets you most of the way.
- **Conversion talk** (MRR, buyers) maps to *your* goal. Decide what that is per video (subs, Etsy/A4Ready traffic, community), or "success" stays undefined and every video looks like a failure.
- **Volume still matters for a solo channel.** The framework improves the hit rate per video, but you only learn from videos you publish. Don't let `/idea-check` turn into a reason not to ship.
